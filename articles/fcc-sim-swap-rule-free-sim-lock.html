Your carrier must offer a free SIM lock by December 17. Turn it on for the phones that approve payments.

The FCC’s SIM swap rules took effect on November 17, 2026, and carriers must comply by December 17. They require a free lock against SIM changes, an alert before any SIM change, and a free way to report fraud. Here is what to switch on this week, and what the rules do not cover yet.

Edited by Emilio Santos November 18, 2026 6 min read

On November 17, 2026, the Federal Communications Commission’s SIM swap rules took effect, three years after the agency adopted them. Mobile carriers must comply by December 17. The part a small business can use right away is simple: every carrier will have to offer a free lock that stops anyone from moving your phone number to a new SIM until you take the lock off.

That matters because many firms still tie money to a phone number. Bank sign-in codes arrive by text message. Payroll and Microsoft 365 accounts fall back to SMS. Vendors call a mobile number to confirm a change in bank details. If a criminal talks a carrier into moving that number to their own SIM, the codes and the calls go to them. The new rules give you a free control and a warning. They take a few minutes per phone to use.

What happened

The Federal Register notice published on November 17 (91 FR 73684) comes from the FCC’s Wireline Competition Bureau. It says the White House Office of Management and Budget approved the paperwork requirements behind the 2023 rules on January 15, 2025, which was the last step before they could take effect. The notice makes the new paragraphs of the FCC’s customer-data rule, 47 CFR 64.2010(h), effective November 17, 2026, and sets December 17, 2026 as the date carriers must comply.

The rules come from the FCC’s SIM Swap and Port-Out Fraud Order, FCC 23-95, released November 16, 2023 and published in the Federal Register on December 8, 2023. They apply to mobile carriers, including resellers that run on another carrier’s network. Under the rules, a carrier must:

  • use secure ways to confirm a customer’s identity before a SIM change, not ones that rely on readily available biographical, account or payment details;
  • have procedures for failed authentication attempts on SIM change requests;
  • notify the customer immediately when a SIM change is requested, before carrying it out;
  • offer every customer, at no cost, the option to lock the account against SIM changes, and not process a SIM change until the customer turns the lock off;
  • describe its account protection measures in clear language on its website and app;
  • give customers a free, easy way to report a fraudulent SIM change, investigate it promptly, and provide documentation on request;
  • keep records of SIM change requests, fraud and the authentication methods used for at least three years;
  • train staff to spot fraudulent SIM change attempts.

What it does not change yet

The 2023 order also wrote parallel rules for “port-outs,” when a number is moved to a different carrier, in 47 CFR 52.37. Those were delayed along with the SIM rules. The November 17 notice names only the SIM change paragraphs, so we do not treat the port-out rules as in force yet. In practice that gap is smaller than it sounds, because the large carriers already offer a separate number transfer lock (see below). It is still worth turning on both.

The rules also do not make SMS codes safe. A lock makes a SIM swap harder; it does not stop phishing pages that ask for the code, or a staff member reading a code out to a caller. The stronger fix is to stop relying on text messages for the accounts that move money.

What the big carriers offer today

We checked the carriers’ own support pages on November 18. Names differ, and business accounts are sometimes managed through a separate portal or account rep:

  • Verizon offers SIM Protection, which blocks SIM and device changes, and Number Lock, which blocks transfers to another carrier. Both are set in the My Verizon app, and Verizon describes Number Lock as free.
  • T-Mobile offers SIM Protection and a separate Port Out Protection, both free. Port Out Protection is added line by line, and only the primary account holder can remove it. Business accounts manage SIM protection in T-Mobile’s Account Hub.
  • AT&T offers Wireless Account Lock, a free setting in the AT&T app that disables specific transactions and account changes for every line on the account. AT&T emails the primary account holder and texts each line whenever it is turned on or off.

If you use a smaller carrier or a reseller, look on its website for an “account protection” page. From December 17, the rules require one.

What it means for CFOs and CISOs

A phone number is often the weakest link in a payment process, and it is controlled by an account most finance teams never look at: the carrier account. Whoever can sign in to that account, or talk a carrier’s support line into a change, can redirect the codes. The new rules shift some of the burden onto carriers, but the lock only helps if someone turns it on and knows who can turn it off.

There is a cost angle too. The lock is free. A fraudulent transfer after a SIM swap is not. A few minutes per phone, on the four or five numbers that approve money, is about as cheap as a control gets. If your cyber insurance application asks how you protect against social engineering, this is a concrete, dated answer you can give.

What to do this week

  1. List the phones that matter. Whose mobile number receives bank, payroll, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace codes, or gets callbacks to approve payments? Usually the owner, the controller, the accounts payable lead and the IT admin.
  2. Turn on the SIM lock and the number transfer lock on each. Write down who can unlock them and how. Put a strong, non-obvious account PIN on the carrier account, and keep it in your password manager.
  3. Move money accounts off SMS where you can. Use an authenticator app or a passkey for banking, payroll and admin accounts. Our password manager guide covers storing the backups.
  4. Add one line to your payment rule. A call about a new number or a “lost phone” never authorizes a payment or a bank change. Confirm on a number already on file. Our guide Stop using a voice as approval has the wording.
  5. Know the warning sign. If a work phone suddenly shows no service or emergency calls only, and the network is fine for everyone else, call the carrier from another phone and check for a SIM change.

If a payment-approving phone still cannot get the free SIM lock, log it with an owner and a review date in our free risk exception register.

What to watch next

  • December 17. The compliance date. Expect carriers to add or rename lock settings and publish account protection pages around then.
  • The port-out paragraphs. Watch for a separate FCC notice making the 47 CFR 52.37 changes effective.
  • Your bank’s options. Ask whether your bank supports app-based or passkey sign-in for business accounts, and whether callbacks can go to a desk line instead of a mobile.
