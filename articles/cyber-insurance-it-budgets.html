Cyber insurance is rewriting the IT budget

Underwriters now ask better questions than some boards. The answers are showing up as line items.

Edited by Emilio Santos August 19, 2026 7 min read Updated October 24, 2026

For a decade, cybersecurity spend followed fear and vendors. Then the insurance market tightened, deductibles jumped, and the renewal packet started to look like an audit. CFOs noticed. The questionnaire was shorter than the strategy deck and meaner about specifics: multifactor everywhere, immutable backups, endpoint detection that is actually watched, a list of who can disable it.

That packet is now one of the more honest IT planning documents in the building. If you cannot get coverage — or you can, but only with a deductible that would wreck a quarter — the control is not optional. It is priced.

From “best practice” to “condition”

MFA on email used to be a talking point. It is now a condition of many policies. So are privileged-access management, tested restores, and a named incident-response retainer. Companies that treated those as a 2028 project discovered they were a 2026 premium.

This is not the insurer being kind. It is the insurer refusing to subsidize the same ransomware script twice. The side effect is useful: it gives the CISO a budget argument that does not depend on a scare story.

The cheapest control is the one that keeps the policy from becoming a brochure.

Where the money actually moves

Watch the mix, not the top line. Identity, backup, and detection are taking share from sprawling “platform” deals that cannot be mapped to a yes/no question on a form. Training still gets funded because underwriters ask about it, even when operators privately doubt the click-through course.

Also watch who owns the relationship. If the broker sits only with finance, IT will be surprised by a control they cannot implement in 30 days. If IT owns it alone, finance will be surprised by the premium. The companies that renew cleanly put both in the room in month nine, not month twelve.

The limit of the model

Insurance is not a substitute for security, and a completed questionnaire is not a mature program. Controls that please an underwriter can still fail a determined attacker. Use the form as a floor. Do not confuse it with a strategy.

Still, as a capital-allocation device, the market is doing something boards failed to do: it is forcing a short list. In a world of infinite tools, a short list is a gift.

Updated October 2026: cheaper policies, same questions

This note first ran on August 19. Since then, the market has kept softening. Our September note, Cyber got cheaper. The questionnaire did not., covers the rate figures and their sources. The short version for budget owners: lower premiums have not removed the control questions. A cheaper policy written on controls you do not actually run can still fail at claim time.

The threat side has not eased. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) reported $16.6 billion in losses reported to it in 2024, a 33% increase from 2023. Business email compromise alone accounted for about $2.77 billion of reported losses. In December 2024 the FBI also warned that criminals use generative AI to make fraud more convincing and to run it at larger scale.

Why it matters, by role

CFO. The insurance application is a short list of controls the market is willing to price. Use it in budget planning. If a control on the form is not in place, the cost shows up somewhere: a higher deductible, an exclusion, a denied claim, or a project you fund in a hurry.

CISO. The form gives you a budget argument that finance already accepts. Map each question to an owner and evidence. “We have MFA” should mean you can show where it is enforced, including email, remote access, admin accounts, and cloud consoles.

Founder. Smaller companies often buy cyber cover because a customer contract requires it. Read what the customer asked for: limit, deductible, and which coverages. Then read the application questions before you answer them. An inaccurate answer is a bigger risk than a higher premium.

Operator. Restores, alert monitoring, and admin account reviews are operations work. If the form says they happen, they need to happen on a schedule, with someone assigned.

The controls that keep coming up

Insurers write their own forms, so wording varies. The controls below line up with public guidance from CISA and NIST, which is a useful way to check that the form is asking for real security and not only paperwork.

  1. Phishing-resistant MFA. CISA’s fact sheet on implementing phishing-resistant MFA identifies FIDO/WebAuthn authenticators and PKI-based methods as the strongest options. Start with email, remote access, and administrators.
  2. Backups you have restored. CISA’s #StopRansomware guide recommends maintaining offline, encrypted backups and regularly testing them. A backup job that has never been restored is an assumption.
  3. Endpoint detection that someone watches. Write down who receives the alert outside business hours.
  4. Privileged access. List who can disable security tools and whether that action is logged.
  5. Incident response plan and contacts. The #StopRansomware guide recommends a plan that is exercised. Know which counsel and forensics firm you would call, and whether the insurer requires its panel.
  6. Payment change verification. Given the business email compromise numbers above, require a call-back to a number already on file before changing vendor bank details. See Stop using a voice as approval.

A planning calendar

  • Month 9 of the policy year. Finance, IT, and the broker review last year’s application. Mark every answer that is not fully true today.
  • Month 10. Fund or schedule the gaps that are cheap to close. Write down the ones that will stay open and why.
  • Month 11. Collect evidence: MFA coverage reports, a recent restore test, the alert escalation list.
  • Month 12. Submit an application you can defend line by line.

The detailed lists are in two guides: the 2026 cyber insurance checklist for IT budgets and What a cyber insurer will still ask. If a control will not be in place by renewal, record it in the risk exception register with an owner and an expiry date.

What to watch next

  • AI-related wording. Watch for new exclusions or endorsements that mention AI, deepfakes, or social engineering. Read where a voice-cloning fraud would fall.
  • Evidence requests. Some underwriters ask for scans or reports, not just yes or no answers. Be ready to show proof.
  • Pricing turns. Soft markets end. Controls you add now will matter more when rates rise again.
