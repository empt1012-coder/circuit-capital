Retail media networks are eating the digital ad market

Closed-loop purchase data beat the open web. The next fight is over who owns the aisle.

Edited by Emilio Santos August 10, 2026 8 min read Updated October 24, 2026

For twenty years the open web sold attention and inferred intent. Retailers already had the receipt. Once they built the pipes to auction that receipt back to brands — onsite search ads, offsite audiences, in-store screens — a new media company appeared inside companies that used to think of themselves as grocers and category killers.

This is why retail media keeps taking budget from both linear TV and the old display stack. Attribution is not a science experiment when you can see the cart. Brands will overpay for a clean story. Retailers will take the overpay and call it high-margin revenue, because it is.

The product is the closed loop

Onsite sponsored search is the core: high intent, ugly creative, excellent conversion. Offsite is the land grab — using loyalty data to follow the shopper onto other apps. In-store is the slow build: screens, shelf labels, receipts. Each layer has different ops. Only the first is mature at most chains.

The strategic risk is concentration. If a handful of retailers become the default path to the basket, brand.com and the open web become a branding tax. That is already the conversation in CPG holding companies, even when the public numbers still look diversified.

The aisle was always media. We just started invoicing it like media.

What brands should demand

Incrementality, not last-click theater. Clean-room access, not a PDF of “insights.” A path to creative that is not ten SKUs fighting in a search box. And a hard look at whether the retailer is also a competitor launching a private label next to the ad you just bought.

Agencies that only know social auctions will struggle. The buying motion looks more like trade promotion plus programmatic, which is a sentence that should frighten anyone with a clean org chart.

The publisher lesson

Independent publishers cannot copy Walmart’s receipt. They can copy the discipline: first-party relationships, honest measurement, and inventory that is scarce enough to price. Retail media’s rise is a reminder that ads follow data gravity. If your site has no gravity — no habit, no identity, no reason to return — you will rent attention at declining rates.

That is the uncomfortable mirror for an ad-supported briefing like this one. The work has to be worth a habit. The rest is slots.

Updated October 2026: the measurement standard is the story

This note first ran on August 10. The point that has held up best is the one about measurement. Retail media is sold on the promise that the retailer can see the sale. The industry now has a public standard for how that should be measured, and buyers should use it.

In January 2024, the Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB) and the Media Rating Council (MRC) published final Retail Media Measurement Guidelines, after a draft for public comment in September 2023. The guidelines cover data collection and quality checks, audience measurement, viewable impressions, invalid traffic, attribution windows, incrementality testing, reporting, and in-store digital screens. The IAB and IAB Europe have also published separate in-store retail media definitions and guidelines. The working group included retailers, brands, agencies, and measurement firms.

Why it matters, by role

CFO. Retail media often sits between two budgets: trade promotion, which finance knows how to audit, and media, which it often does not. Decide which budget it comes from and apply that budget’s controls. If it is trade spend with a media label, it should still have to show an effect on sales that would not have happened anyway.

Founder. For a young consumer brand, sponsored search on a large retailer can be the fastest way to be found. It can also become a toll you pay forever. Track how much of your revenue on that retailer depends on ads. If the share keeps rising, the retailer has more pricing power over you each quarter.

Operator. Retail media ties ads to inventory. If you advertise a product that is out of stock, you pay for clicks that cannot convert. Connect the ad schedule to your stock feed, or at least check it weekly.

CISO. Clean rooms and audience sharing move customer data between companies. Ask what data leaves your systems, under which contract, and who can query it. A clean room is a control only if you know its rules.

What to ask a retail media network

  1. Incrementality. Which method does the network use to show that sales would not have happened anyway? The IAB/MRC guidelines describe randomized controlled tests, synthetic controls, matched-market tests, and modeled approaches. Ask which one, and ask for the methodology in writing.
  2. Attribution window. How many days after an ad view or click does a sale count? Longer windows produce bigger numbers. Make sure you compare networks on the same window.
  3. Invalid traffic. How is non-human or invalid traffic filtered, and is that filtering audited?
  4. Viewability. For display and video, what counts as a viewable impression? For in-store screens, how is exposure measured?
  5. Reporting granularity. Can you see results by product, by placement, and by new versus repeat customer, or only a summary?
  6. Data access. What can you take away from a clean room, and what stays with the retailer?
  7. Conflicts. Does the retailer sell a private-label product in the same category? If so, how are placement and data walls handled?

A simple test you can run

If the network cannot offer a controlled test, run a crude one yourself. Pick a set of comparable products or regions. Pause ads on half for a few weeks and keep the other half running. Compare sales. This is less rigorous than the methods in the guidelines, and seasonality and promotions can distort it. It is still better than accepting attributed sales as incremental sales.

What to watch next

  • Audited measurement. Whether networks have their measurement audited against MRC standards, and say so in their sales materials.
  • In-store screens. In-store media is the least mature layer. Watch how exposure is counted before paying premium prices for it.
  • Concentration. Whether a few retailers keep gaining share of brand budgets. That shifts pricing power away from brands and toward the retailers that own the checkout.
