How to tell AI spend from AI return

The industry is writing enormous checks. Most firms still cannot put EBIT on the same slide. That is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to measure one workflow.

By Emilio Santos Updated September 4, 2026 10 min read

Who this is for

The U.S. mid-market CFO, founder, or operator who will not sign another copilot bill without a definition of done — and the director who has to ask the next question in the board pack.

On this page

  1. Seat cost versus token cost
  2. Pilot versus production
  3. Questions a board can ask

Gartner’s May 2026 forecast put worldwide AI spending at $2.59 trillion this year, up 47%, with infrastructure more than 45% of the pile. John-David Lovelock’s line is the one that belongs in a mid-market packet: so far the spend is driven by vendors and hyperscalers. Enterprises “have yet to really flex their spending potential,” and CIOs still struggle to show a business outcome.

McKinsey’s 2026 State of AI survey still had only 37% of respondents attributing any EBIT to AI — roughly unchanged — and about 6% as high performers (significant impact and at least 5% of EBIT). Eighty percent said AI improved their own productivity. Those two facts can both be true. A faster draft is not a cheaper close. This page is how you stop mixing them. It is not a promise that a tool will pay for itself.

Seat cost versus token cost

Two invoices get lumped as “AI.” They do not behave the same way.

Seat / copilot license Tokens / usage
What you buy A named human, a month at a time Work the model actually did
How it hides Shelfware. A seat that never opens Retries, long context, agents looping
What to count Active seats versus paid seats, on one workflow Spend on that workflow only — not the company pile
What not to count “Everyone should have one” The hyperscaler capex story on a keynote slide

McKinsey found about 20% of respondents already saying AI operating costs, including tokens, constrained use — while most still planned to spend more. If the only metric is seats, the organization will buy seats. If the only metric is tokens, it will run them without a finish line.

Add a third cost that never appears on the vendor invoice: human review after the draft, and rework when the draft invents a date, a customer, or a covenant. Time saved at the desk does not show up as margin if the review step and the headcount plan do not change.

Pilot versus production

A pilot has a demo. Production has a definition of done, an owner, and a number that can move. Gartner said in September that only 22% of organizations had successfully scaled AI across multiple business units. You do not need to be in the 22%. You need one artifact you can date.

Good production tests: monthly close commentary, support macros, bid/no-bid memos, invoice exceptions. Bad: “make the company more AI-native.” If you cannot name the artifact that is done, you are still in the pilot, whatever the slide says.

Write the current path: who starts it, how long it takes, where it fails, what “done” looks like. That is the baseline. No baseline, no after. Ninety days, one owner whose bonus includes cycle time or error rate. If nothing moved, cut the overlapping copilots and keep the model on the one path that has an owner.

A budget that grows 47% while the EBIT share stays flat is not a strategy. It is a bet that the spreadsheet will catch up.

Questions a board can ask

Directors do not need a model architecture briefing. They need a control framework that survives the next vendor cycle.

  1. Which single workflow is in production, and what is the definition of done?
  2. What did that workflow cost last quarter in seats, tokens, and review time — separately?
  3. What number moved: cycle time, error rate, or headcount plan? “People feel faster” is not a number.
  4. Who can turn the spend off, and who owns the data the model can see?
  5. What happens if the vendor changes the price or the model next quarter — is there an exit, or only a renewal?
  6. Are we in the 37% who can attribute any EBIT, or are we buying seats because the industry forecast is $2.59 trillion?

None of those questions imply a guaranteed return. They imply a company that can tell spend from a result. If the packet cannot answer them, the next copilot is a catalog item, not an operating change.

FAQ

Will this pay for itself?

This page does not say that. Most surveyed firms still cannot put EBIT on the slide. Measure one workflow. If the number does not move, stop buying seats for that path.

Is a copilot seat “AI return”?

Only if a named process got cheaper or faster with the review step counted. A license that is not opened is spend.

Do we need to match Gartner’s $2.59 trillion?

No. That pile is mostly infrastructure and hyperscalers. Your job is one definition of done.

What if tokens are already constraining use?

Then you are not under-adopting. You are over-running a process with no finish line. Cap the workflow, not the slogan.
