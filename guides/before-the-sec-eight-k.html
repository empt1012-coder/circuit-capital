Before the Form 8-K clock

The SEC’s clock is four business days from the materiality determination. Write down who can make that call, and what a private vendor must be able to say.

Edited by Emilio Santos October 1, 2026 8 min read Updated October 24, 2026

Who this is for

A public company’s incident lead, and the private vendor who will be asked for facts on that company’s clock. Not legal advice. Not a materiality conclusion.

The SEC’s small-entity guide says domestic registrants disclose a material cybersecurity incident on Form 8-K within four business days after they determine the incident is material. Item 1.05 asks for the material aspects of nature, scope, and timing, and the material impact or reasonably likely material impact. The annual Form 10-K, under Regulation S-K Item 106, is a different duty: processes, board oversight, and management’s role.

The clock

Do not start the four days at the first alert. The Commission’s guide starts them at the materiality determination. That only helps if someone is authorized to make the determination, and if the facts they need are not trapped in a vendor’s ticket queue. Discovery without a decision is how the week vanishes.

What to rehearse

  • Name the person who can call an incident material, and the counsel they call. A committee that meets monthly will miss four business days.
  • List the facts Item 1.05 points at: what happened, what it touched, when, and what it does to the business. Practice filling those from logs you already keep.
  • If you are the vendor, write the notice you can send in one business day: systems, data types, what you turned off, what you do not yet know. “Promptly” in a contract is slower than a customer’s 8-K.
  • Keep the annual Item 106 description current. The incident form and the annual description answer different questions. The Commission did not require disclosure of a director’s cybersecurity expertise. Do not add that as if it were Item 106.

What the rule says, in more detail

The SEC adopted its cybersecurity disclosure rules on July 26, 2023. For a material cybersecurity incident, Item 1.05 of Form 8-K requires a company to describe the material aspects of the nature, scope, and timing of the incident, and its material impact or reasonably likely material impact, including on financial condition and results of operations. The filing is generally due four business days after the company determines the incident is material. The company must make that determination without unreasonable delay after discovery.

The rule allows a delay if the US Attorney General determines that disclosure would pose a substantial risk to national security or public safety and tells the SEC in writing. That is a narrow path, not a planning assumption.

On May 21, 2024, Erik Gerding, then Director of the SEC’s Division of Corporation Finance, issued a statement clarifying that Item 1.05 is for incidents the company has determined to be material. A company that wants to disclose an incident it has not determined to be material, or has determined is not material, can use a different item, such as Item 8.01. That keeps Item 1.05 meaningful for investors.

A tabletop drill in five steps

  1. Pick a realistic scenario. For example, ransomware encrypts a billing system, or a vendor reports that your customer data was exposed.
  2. Start the clock at discovery. Time how long it takes for the facts to reach the person who owns the materiality decision.
  3. Make the determination. Have the decision owner and counsel walk through the thresholds the company uses: financial, operational, customer, legal, reputational. Record the evidence considered.
  4. Count four business days. Draft the 8-K language for nature, scope, timing, and impact using only the facts you have.
  5. Review what was slow. Usually it is getting facts from a vendor, or finding the right decision-maker. Fix that before a real incident.

A worked example of the clock

Illustrative dates. An incident is discovered on Thursday, October 1, 2026. After investigation, the company determines on Tuesday, October 6, that it is material. The four business days start the next day: Wednesday the 7th, Thursday the 8th, Friday the 9th. Monday, October 12 is Columbus Day, a federal holiday, so it is skipped. The filing is due Tuesday, October 13, 2026. The discovery date does not move it. The 8-K materiality worksheet counts this for you and keeps a record of who decided what.

If you are the vendor

Your customer’s clock depends on your facts. Agree in advance on what you will send within one business day of an incident that may affect them: affected systems, data types, the time window, containment steps, and what is still unknown. Name who sends it. Put it in the contract if the customer asks. A vague “we will notify promptly” is slower than four business days in practice.

Before you sign off

  • One named person owns the materiality decision, with a named deputy.
  • The thresholds the company uses are written down before an incident.
  • Key vendors know what facts to send you, and how fast.
  • The team has run at least one tabletop and fixed what was slow.
  • The annual Item 106 description matches how the process actually works.

None of this is legal advice. Securities counsel should review the process and any draft filing.

FAQ

We are private. Why rehearse?

Because a public customer’s counsel will ask you for the facts in their filing window. Your own Form 8-K is not the only clock in the room.

Does insurance decide materiality?

No. An underwriter’s question and Item 1.05 are different documents. Answer both from the same facts. Do not let one substitute for the other.
